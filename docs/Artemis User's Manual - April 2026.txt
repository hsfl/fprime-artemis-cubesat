1.2. Intended Audience
The Artemis CubeSat Kit is intended for educators and students of the University-level. The goal of Artemis CubeSat Kit is to provide educational institutions with accessibility to a low-cost, spaceflight-ready small-satellite. The kit creates unique opportunities for educators to integrate their curriculum with hands-on project-based learning. The Artemis team created its curriculum with a purpose to enhance university-level students’ STEM and aerospace knowledge. In addition, the Artemis CubeSat is designed to be user-friendly and can be easily assembled and integrated for launch into space. After initial setup and configuration by the end-user, the CubeSat can become operational and begin performing its intended functions.


The Artemis CubeSat Kit has the potential to reach a diverse range of audiences. For instance, Project POKE, which is geared towards middle and high school-level students, uses the Artemis CubeSat Kit as the engineering model baseline. Another example is Ke Ao, a 1U CubeSat that is being developed by Team Laniākea, an undergraduate student team at the University of Hawaii at Manoa, in collaboration with the Hawaii Space Flight Laboratory. Ke Ao also uses the Artemis CubeSat Kit as a baseline, but has a distinct mission objective of utilizing machine learning algorithms to capture images of the Hawaiian Islands from space for atmospheric observation purposes.

The foundational concepts, assembly, and integration of the Artemis CubeSat Kit is thoroughly explained in this document. However, the building of a 1U CubeSat may be complex for a new user, or one unfamiliar with the topics. If you encounter any difficulties in understanding the instructions listed in this document or have any technical questions, please do not hesitate to contact the Hawaii Space Flight Laboratory and the Artemis team for assistance. 1.4. Acronyms
There are several terms throughout this document that can be abbreviated for convenience and consistency. If you're looking to gain a deeper understanding of these terms, the Artemis CubeSat Kit textbook is an excellent resource to consider. Below are acronyms and its respective description that the user should familiarize themselves with:


General Definitions
* PCB - Printed Circuit Board
   * A PCB is a fundamental component of most electronic devices, including the Artemis CubeSat. It serves as a platform to connect and support electronic components, such as resistors, capacitors, and integrated circuits, by routing conductive pathways through layers of non-conductive substrate material.
   * PCBs come in various shapes and sizes, with an outer diameter (OD) representing the overall size of the board. For example, in the context of a 1U CubeSat, the PCBs have an OD of 100mm x 100mm, as specified by the CubeSat standard.
   * PCBs can have multiple layers of conductive pathways, and each layer can be interconnected using vias or holes drilled through the board. The number of layers in a PCB depends on the complexity of the system and the number of electronic components that need to be connected. In the case of the Artemis CubeSat, its PCBs use up to 4 layers to accommodate the necessary components and routing.
* BOM - Bill of Materials
   * A Bill of Materials (BOM) is an essential document that enumerates all the electronic components, such as ICs, resistors, capacitors, and more, required for the construction of a PCB. Every PCB in the Artemis CubeSat Kit is accompanied by a BOM that outlines the exact quantities, values, and other crucial information about each component. The BOM serves as a reference point for PCB assembly and testing, and can be used to organize component purchases from various vendors.
* IC - Integrated Circuit
   * IC is often referred to as a microelectronic circuit, microchip, or chip. It is a fundamental device that is made up of several electronic circuits, which work together as a single unit. The Artemis CubeSat Kit is composed of many ICs.


Artemis CubeSat Kit Subsystems
* COMMs - Communications
   * The COMMs subsystem is a vital component of the Artemis CubeSat responsible for enabling communication between the satellite and the ground station. Associated with COMMs are the Ground Support Equipment (GSE), radio, and and the radio antenna, which facilitate the transmission and reception of data and commands between the CubeSat and the ground station in space.
* ADCS - Attitude Determination and Control System
   * The ADCS is a significant subsystem of the Artemis CubeSat responsible for maintaining the satellite’s orientation or attitude while in space. The ADCS achieves this through a combination of sensors, actuators, and algorithms that work together to determine and regulate the CubeSat’s attitude, ensuring it remains stable and properly aligned with its designated trajectory.
* EPS - Electrical Power System
   * The EPS is an important subsystem of the Artemis CubeSat that comprises the power distribution unit (PDU), battery board and batteries, and solar panels and cells, all of which work together to distribute and regulate power throughout the CubeSat.
* OBC - On-Board Computer
   * The OBC is a critical subsystem of the Artemis CubeSat, serving as a dedicated board that includes both hardware and software components. Together, these components work in tandem to establish communication with the other subsystems within the satellite. The OBC is programmable and can be configured to monitor and control various functionalities of the small satellite mission.
* FSW - Flight Software
   * The FSW is a key software component of the OBC subsystem of the Artemis CubeSat. The FSW plays a vital role in the operation of the CubeSat during its mission in space. Specifically, it facilitates the movement and control of the satellite, ensuring its proper flight trajectory and functioning. As a central component of the OBC, the FSW is responsible for managing and coordinating the various subsystems of the CubeSat, including the COMMs, ADCS, and EPS, among others.
* PLD - Payload
   * The Payload (PLD) in the Artemis CubeSat Kit is an important component that carries out the specific mission objectives of the CubeSat. In the case of the Artemis CubeSat Kit, the PLD includes a functional camera with AI feature matching capabilities. This camera can capture images of the Earth, stars, or other objects in space, and the AI feature matching can help identify and track those objects. The PLD can be customized to suit the specific mission needs of the CubeSat, making it a crucial component for a successful space mission.
* Structure and Mechanisms - S&M
   * The S&M subsystem plays a critical role in the Artemis CubeSat Kit. It is responsible for designing, developing, and implementing the spacecraft structure and mechanical systems. This includes ensuring that the structure and mechanisms comply with the rigorous requirements of launch vehicles and the harsh space environment. 
   * The S&M subsystem is also responsible for the design and deployment of the antenna mechanism. The Artemis CubeSat uses a nichrome burn wire for the antenna deployment mechanism, which is a reliable and proven method for small satellite deployments. 
* Thermal Control System - TCS
   * The TCS subsystem is responsible for regulating and stabilizing temperature changes within the CubeSat during orbit. This is crucial for ensuring the proper functioning of all the electronic components and systems within the CubeSat. 
   * In the Artemis CubeSat Kit, the thermal control subsystem is implemented through the use of passive thermal control techniques such as radiation and conduction. The CubeSat's exterior is coated with a specialized material that helps to reflect heat and radiation away from the spacecraft. 
   * Additionally, the thermal control subsystem includes temperature sensors to monitor and regulate the temperature within the CubeSat. 
   * In addition, the TCS subsystem in the Artemis CubeSat Kit includes a Kapton Heater attached to the batteries. The Kapton Heater regulates the temperature of the batteries to ensure that they do not get too hot. This is important because if the battery temperature is not regulated, it can affect the performance and lifespan of the battery. The Kapton Heater is a thin and flexible heater made of Kapton material, which is a polyimide film known for its high temperature resistance
Featured Elements
* GSE - Ground Support Equipment
   * GSE refers to the various equipment and tools required to support the operation and maintenance of a 1U CubeSat on the ground. GSE is necessary for tasks such as CubeSat integration, testing, and deployment.
   * GSE is typically used by the ground station team, who are responsible for communicating with the CubeSat and controlling its operation while it is in orbit. Properly designed and functioning GSE is essential for ensuring that the CubeSat can be operated safely and effectively, and for maximizing the scientific or engineering return of the mission.
* IMU - Inertial Measurement Unit [ADCS]
   * The IMU is a crucial device within the OBC that utilizes a combination of sensors to measure and report the CubeSat's orientation, acceleration, and angular velocity. This data is essential for the proper operation of the ADCS subsystem, as it provides the necessary information for determining and maintaining the spacecraft's position and attitude in space.
* VBATT - Battery Voltage [EPS]
   * VBatt refers to the power provided by the lithium-ion batteries (Sanyo NCR18650GA), which are housed on the Artemis CubeSat battery board. The batteries are charged by the solar panel. VBATT has a minimum of 6 V and maximum of 7.2 V at any given time. On average, VBATT provides a constant 6.2 V to 6.8 V to the Artemis CubeSat.
* PDU - Power Distribution Unit [EPS]
   * The PDU is part of the EPS, and is a board in the Artemis CubeSat Kit. The PDU is responsible for regulating, controlling, and distributing power to the rest of the CubeSat. It includes key features such as a microcontroller (MCU), power buses, and switches, which enable it to take in battery power and regulate it into a suitable voltage supply line for the components.
* MCU - Microcontroller Unit [EPS]
   * The MCU is a crucial programmable component located on the PDU. Its primary function is to manage and regulate various circuits within the CubeSat, including communication with other PCBs, data processing, and more.
* IBF - Insert Before Flight [EPS]
   * The IBF circuit is a crucial component of the PDU board in a CubeSat. It enables the disconnection and reconnection of power to the satellite by means of an IBF pin. When the pin is inserted, power is supplied, and the CubeSat's electronic components are operational. Conversely, when the IBF pin is disconnected, power is cut off from the CubeSat, and its electronic components are not operational. This feature is essential for the control, testing, and integration of the small satellite before and after it is launched into space.
* CSI - Camera Serial Interface [PLD]
   * CSI is a protocol used for communication between a camera module and a host device. In the Artemis CubeSat, the CSI interface is used to connect the Raspberry Pi camera module payload to the OBC. The CSI interface allows for high-speed data transfer between the camera and the processing unit, enabling the capture and processing of high-resolution images or video.


Communication Interfaces
* GPIO - General Purpose Input Output
   * A GPIO is a digital signal pin that can be programmed by the user. A GPIO does not have any function until it is programmed to work as instructed, such as handling input, output, or both digital signals. For example, a GPIO can be programmed to allow communication between two devices or read digital data from sensors. GPIOs are present in devices such as the MCU.
* I2C - Inter-Integrated Circuit
   * I2C is a type of protocol that allows communication between two or more ICs. Generally, it involves one device to act as Master and another device to act as Slave. There can only be one Master, but there can be multiple Slaves. The Master sends data to Slave. The Slave receives the data from Master, and sometimes sends back data to Master. 
* SDA -  Serial Data Line & SCL - Serial Clock Line
   * SDA and SCL are the lines for I2C communication. SDA allows for Master and Slave to both send and receive data. SCL carries the clock signal and can only be sent from Master to Slave. In digital circuits, clock signals are used to synchronize and coordinate actions.
* SPI - Serial Peripheral Interface
   * SPI is a type of communication, most commonly between microcontrollers (computer) and peripheral IC devices. Peripheral IC devices input data into the microcontroller, and receive the data output from the microcontroller.
   * There is a SPI Master (microcontroller) and SPI Slave (peripheral IC devices). Similar to I2C, there can only be one Master, but there can be multiple Slaves. 
   * SPI has four lines of communication:
      * SCLK (Serial Clock) - Master sends clock signal data to Slave
      * MOSI (Master Out Slave In) - Master sends data to Slave
      * MISO (Master In Slave Out) - Slave sends data to Master
      * CS or SS (Chip Select or Slave Select) - Master sends data to Slave indicating whether data was sent
* UART - Universal Asynchronous Receiver-Transmitter
   * UART is a type of protocol that allows direct communication between two devices. There are two lines of communication in UART: TX (Transmit) and RX (Receive).
   * When data is transmitted from Device 1, it is sent through its TX pin and received by Device 2's RX pin. Similarly, when data is transmitted from Device 2, it is sent through its TX pin and received by Device 1's RX pin.
   * TX converts parallel data from the controller into a serial form, and sends it to RX. RX will receive the data and convert the serial data back into the parallel data for usage.
   * UART works differently from I2C and SPI because it does not have a clock signal for synchronization. Therefore, the transmitted and received data behave asynchronously, such that UART adds bits to the beginning and end of a packet (transmitted and received data) that tells it when to synchronize the data. UART can be programmed to work at different speeds.
Contextual Definitions
* KCC - Kauai Community College
   * The Artemis CubeSat Kit is being developed with KCC serving as its ground station for the link budget. For their space missions, end users have the flexibility to choose their own ground station.
      * What is a Link Budget?
         * A link budget is a calculation that takes into account all of the gains and losses from the transmitter, the medium that the signal travels through (such as free space, fiber optic cable, or atmosphere), and the receiver. It is used to determine the maximum range of a communication link, or the maximum data rate that can be achieved over a given range. A link budget is an important tool for designing and optimizing communication systems.
* CSLI - CubeSat Launch Initiative
   * CSLI is a program run by NASA to provide opportunities for universities, non-profit organizations, and other groups to launch CubeSats into space at low cost. The Artemis CubeSat Kit was designed in accordance with CSLI guidelines, which ensures that the CubeSat meets specific size and weight requirements for launch, as well as technical specifications for communications and power systems.

2. Introduction
The Artemis CubeSat kit comprises three primary subsystems: Avionics, Mechanical, and Software, each carefully designed to address crucial aspects of our comprehensive system.


Within the Avionics subsystem, focus revolves around the design, implementation, and rigorous testing of diverse electronic circuit boards residing within the CubeSat. This intricate process ensures that every individual component seamlessly integrates into the cohesive whole, harmonizing to fulfill the system's overarching objectives.


The Mechanical subsystem takes charge of the satellite's physical structure, undertaking the crucial task of crafting a robust housing. This protective enclosure is designed to shield and contain all electronic components, meticulously considering the challenges posed by the demanding space environment.


Meanwhile, the Software subsystem delves into the operational intricacies of the satellite, covering crucial aspects such as communication with the ground station. This subsystem acts as a vital conduit between the satellite and Earth, orchestrating seamless interaction.


In essence, the Artemis CubeSat comprises three interwoven systems: Avionics, Mechanical, and Software. The Avionics system seamlessly integrates key subsystems such as the On-Board Computer System (OBCS), Electrical Power System (EPS), and COMMs (Communications), alongside the Thermal Control System (TCS), Attitude Determination and Control Systems (ADCS), and Payload. The Mechanical system provides a robust structural foundation for the space raft, while the Software component encompasses both the flight software executing on the satellite and the test software, ensuring comprehensive operational capabilities.

2.1. Avionics


The Artemis CubeSat's Avionics System serves as the central hub for the satellite's intricate operations. Comprising essential subsystems, it includes the Electrical Power System (EPS), On-Board Computer (OBC), and COMMs for communication, along with the Thermal Control System (TCS), Attitude Determination and Control Systems (ADCS), and Payload. The EPS manages power distribution, the OBC coordinates tasks, COMMs ensures communication with Earth, the TCS regulates temperature, the ADCS maintains proper orientation, and the Payload carries mission-specific instruments. This system represents a precisely crafted fusion of technologies, essential for orchestrating the CubeSat's mission in the demanding environment of space.

2.2. Mechanical


The Artemis CubeSat, relies on a mechanical subsystem that serves as the backbone of its structural stability and integrity. This pivotal component plays a crucial role throughout the spacecraft's entire lifecycle, navigating through the challenges of harsh launch conditions and the demanding space environment. Tasked with the mechanical design, fabrication, and testing of the CubeSat's frame and housing, this subsystem securely houses internal components.


Designed in adherence to the CubeSat standard of small-sized cubic units (U), with dimensions of 10x10x10 cm, the mechanical subsystem incorporates lightweight yet robust and rigid materials, optimizing the limited space available, ensuring the CubeSat's overall functionality.


The mechanical subsystem of the Artemis CubeSat goes beyond its role in structural support; it is a key player in ensuring compliance with the specifications of the launch vehicle provider. This is particularly critical, as the CubeSat must endure violent vibrations and loads during launch without compromising the safety of the rocket or other payloads. Rigorous structural and thermal analyses, coupled with extensive testing and qualification procedures, validate the robustness of the mechanical design, guaranteeing the spacecraft's reliability under extreme conditions.
2.3. Software


At its core, this architecture comprises two integral components – the flight software governing the satellite's autonomous operations and the testing software that plays a pivotal role in ensuring the reliability of each subsystem. Together, these components form a cohesive system that not only orchestrates the CubeSat's functions in the vast expanse of space but also undergoes rigorous testing to guarantee its readiness for the complexities of its mission.


The flight software emerges as the brain behind the Artemis CubeSat, imbued with the capability to manage a myriad of tasks crucial to the satellite's functioning. From data processing to communication protocols, this software optimally synchronizes the operations of the Avionics subsystem. In doing so, it not only ensures efficiency but also adapts seamlessly to the dynamic space environment.


Concurrently, the testing software assumes a critical role during the developmental and pre-flight phases, serving as the litmus test for the CubeSat's reliability. By validating the functionality of each subsystem through a series of rigorous testing scenarios, this software system ensures that the satellite is well-prepared to confront the challenges of its mission.
The flight software architecture of the Artemis CubeSat has been meticulously designed to efficiently handle and process the extensive data generated by the physical components of the kit. This prowess enables the flight software to collect telemetry, process data, and issue commands to control hardware components, thus facilitating a seamless and autonomous execution of the CubeSat's mission objectives.


Delving into the intricacies of Artemis’ testing software, it offers users a valuable toolkit to explore and understand the diverse components of the CubeSat. The on-board computer (OBC) features a Teensy-driven Artemis Arduino library, complete with user-friendly code crafted for testing sensors and other embedded elements. This library, by deconstructing the flight software into individual components, empowers users to rigorously test and evaluate the functionality of different systems and elements, fostering a deeper understanding of their satellite. Facilitating this testing software is the Arduino programming language, a framework rooted in C and C++, utilizing the Arduino IDE for coding Arduino board microcontrollers.

4. On Board Computer


The On-Board Computer (OBC) of the Artemis CubeSat serves as a central nervous system, essential for the satellite's functionality. Comprising a microcontroller, microprocessor, sensors, GPS module, and radio communication module, the OBC is a versatile and adaptable component designed to facilitate effective communication among various satellite subsystems.
Embedded within the Artemis CubeSat, the OBC is a blend of both hardware and software. Its programming enables it to monitor the satellite's health and regulate mission-critical functionalities. Equipped with general-purpose features, it allows end-users immediate deployment while offering the flexibility for redesign and reprogramming to suit specific mission requirements.
Functionally, the OBC is the linchpin connecting sensor- and data-related components within the Artemis CubeSat. Current and temperature sensors provide essential data on voltage flow and temperature, while programmable devices, including the Teensy 4.1, Raspberry Pi Zero, flight GPS, IMU, and modular radio, enable control over the satellite's actions and behaviors.
The Teensy 4.1, which collects data and communicates commands to the Power Distribution Unit (PDU) microcontroller. This communication stream facilitates the seamless activation or deactivation of various components, demonstrating the OBC's crucial role in managing the satellite's operations.


4.1 Microcontroller: 
4.1.1 Teensy 4.1


The Teensy 4.1 is a powerful and compact microcontroller board designed for those seeking high-performance capabilities in a small form factor. Developed by PJRC, the Teensy 4.1 builds upon the success of its predecessors, offering a significant upgrade in terms of processing power and features.


At the heart of the Teensy 4.1 is a 600 MHz ARM Cortex-M7 processor, providing a substantial boost in computational capabilities compared to earlier models. This increased clock speed opens up a wide range of possibilities for demanding applications, making it suitable for embedded system projects. 


With a generous amount of RAM (8 MB) and Flash memory (2 MB), the Teensy 4.1 provides ample space for storing code and handling data-intensive tasks. The board also boasts a variety of connectivity options, including USB, I2C, SPI, and multiple serial ports, allowing for seamless integration with a plethora of sensors, displays, and other peripherals.


One standout feature of the Teensy 4.1 is its compatibility with the Arduino development environment, making it accessible to a broad community of developers familiar with Arduino programming. Additionally, its compact size, measuring just 2.4 x 0.7 inches, makes it suitable for projects with space constraints.

Functional Flow:
   1. Startup
   * When the CubeSat powers on, Pin 18 supplies regulated 3.3V from the EPS.
   * Pin 2 (VBATT) is already powered by the Teensy, preserving RTC and SRAM.

   * Pin 1 (RSTN) is controlled by Teensy GPIO 33.

   3. Signal Acquisition
   * Pin 32 (ANT_ receives GNSS signals via an external antenna connected through an MCX cable.

   * Once locked it calculates position, velocity, and time using onboard algorithms.
   4. Data Transmission
   * Pin 44 (TXDO) sends GNSS data to Teensy via UART5_RX (Pin 29).
   * Pin 42 (RXD0) received configuration commands from Teensy via UART5_TX (Pin 28).
   * Data includes position (latitude, longitude, altitude), velocity (speed, heading), time (UTC, fix timestamp)
   * Teensy parses this data and can log it.

4.3.4 Hope RF RFM23BPS Radio
The Hope RF RFM23BPS is a high power, highly flexible, highly integrated, low cost wireless ISM RF transceiver module. This module ensures extended range and improved link performance, making it ideal for satellite communication. The RFM23BPS is a high power, modular radio intended for spaceflight on the Artemis CubeSat Kit. The RFM23BPS radio transmits in either FSK, GFSK, or OOK; we intend to transmit in either FSK or GFSK.  Transmission up to 125kbs is possible with the RFM23BPS, however, we intend to transmit at 2kbs to ensure transmission reliability. The Artemis team chose the RFM23BPS radio because it can transmit at +30dBm (1W) and because it uses the non-proprietary GFSK modulation scheme. The RFM23BPS is controlled by the Teensy 4.1 through Serial Peripheral Interface (SPI). The radio software is powered by RadioHead library, and comes included with the teensyduino package. 


Key Specifications:
   * Frequency Range: 433/868/915MHz ISM bands
   * Sensitivity: –120 dBm
   * Output power: +30 dBm Max
   * Low Power Consumption: 25 mA receive, 550 mA @ +30 dBm transmit
   * Data Rate: 0.123 to 256 kbps
   * Modulation: FSK, GFSK, and OOK
   * Power Supply: 3.3 to 6 V
   * Temperature Range: –40 to +85 °C
   * Features: Digital RSSI, Wake-up timer, Auto-frequency calibration (AFC), Power-on-reset (POR), Antenna diversity and TR switch control, Configurable packet handler, Preamble detector, TX and RX 64 byte FIFOs, Low battery detector, Temperature sensor and 8-bit ADC, Integrated voltage regulators, Frequency hopping capability, On-chip crystal tuning​.


The RFM23BPS radio requires three fundamental components for its operation: a power supply, a digital connection to a controller like the Teensy 4.1, and an antenna.


The power requirements are straightforward, requiring both a hot feed of 3.3V or 5V and a grounding connection. This supplies the energy that the radio needs to carry out its functions.
The second requirement is a digital communication line to a controller, which is achieved through a SPI (Serial Peripheral Interface) connection to a Teensy 4.1. This interface typically incorporates four connections - the serial data in, serial data out, clock, and chip select. However, in the case of the RFM23 radio, an additional four digital pins are utilized, totalling eight pins for the connection. These include the interrupt, radio reset, TXon, and RXon pins. Of these, the interrupt pin is crucial, for without it, the radio is incapable of receiving messages.

        •        To transmit: Set TX_ON = LOW, RX_ON = HIGH
        •        To receive: Set TX_ON = HIGH, RX_ON = LOW
These pins use active-low logic—setting a pin LOW turns that amplifier ON. Failure to configure these pins will result in severely degraded performance.​​​​​​​​​​​​​​​​


A noteworthy detail is that both radios share the rightmost set of solder pads, implying that only one radio can be mounted on the board at any given moment. The shared solder pads also mean the power pad remains the same for both radios, leading to a bit of a conundrum.
The RFM23BPS operates at 5V to transmit at full power, although it can also function at a reduced transmission power level using 3V3. In contrast, the RFM69HCW exclusively operates on 3V3. Per the manufacturer's datasheet, exposing the RFM69HCW to 5V can cause irreversible damage.


To circumvent this power supply issue, we've integrated resistors, indicated by the red circle, to provide a versatile solution. These resistors allow the user to select the appropriate power supply for the radio, thereby catering to both potential requirements. However, it's crucial to avoid populating both simultaneously as it would inadvertently connect a 5V power supply to a 3V3 power supply, creating a problematic scenario.


In the OBC version 4.24, two zero-ohm resistors establish a connection between the radio and either the 3.3V switch or the 3.3V bus. However, it's worth noting that the labeling on the board itself is misleading. Specifically, in the OBC version 4.24, the resistor denoted as 5V is, in fact, the 3V3 switch 2. The resistor for the 3V3 bus, thankfully, carries the correct label.




For OBC Version 4.23, there is a hardware issue where the 5V bus is not connected at all. We can confirm the 3V3 bus works to turn on the radio. The RFM23BPS will operate safely at 3.3 V, but its Power Amplifier output falls from +30 dBm to roughly +17–20 dBm (50–100 mW), so you’ll lose range compared to a 5 V supply. For in-room bench testing over a few meters, that reduced power is still more than sufficient if you maintain proper antenna spacing, common grounding, and a stable 3.3 V regulator. 


We recommend employing the 3V3 switch to power the radio, as this facilitates manual resets during flight. It's important for users to keep in mind that when the kit is activated, 3V3 switch 2 might require manual activation via the pdu_comm program in the Artemis-Cubesat Arduino Library. This is a crucial step to ensure the efficient operation of the system.


If testing the radio via the artemis-cubesat examples of radio_receive_test.ino/radio_send_test.ino ensure that ANTJ15 is plugged into the OBC J12 so that the radio is properly initialized.

5. Electrical Power System


The Electrical Power System (EPS) serves as a critical foundation within the Artemis CubeSat, seamlessly integrating essential components to efficiently manage and regulate power throughout its operation in orbit. Comprising the Power Distribution Unit (PDU), Batteries with their dedicated battery board, and Solar Panels with multiple solar cells, the EPS is designed to generate, store, and distribute power to various subsystems within the CubeSat.


At the heart of the EPS lies the Power Distribution Unit (PDU), a key orchestrator responsible for redirecting and utilizing VBattery (VBATT). The Batteries and Battery Board, featuring Sanyo NCR18650GA batteries as the primary power source in the form of VBATT, play a central role in supplying power to the CubeSat during its orbit. Simultaneously, the Solar Panels harness electromagnetic radiation from the sun, converting it into electrical energy through their solar cells. This energy is then utilized to recharge the batteries, ensuring a continuous and reliable power supply.


In orbit, the batteries take center stage, delivering VBATT to power the CubeSat, while the PDU efficiently manages the distribution of this power across diverse systems within the satellite. The Solar Panels, with their adept solar cells, contribute to the rejuvenation of the batteries by converting solar energy into electrical energy during the satellite's journey through space.
The cohesive functioning of these EPS components exemplifies meticulous design and integration, forming vital working circuits that collaboratively contribute to the overall functionality of the Artemis CubeSat. In essence, the Electrical Power System stands as a testament to precision engineering, ensuring an uninterrupted power supply for the successful execution of the CubeSat's mission.


5.1. PDU


The PDU is responsible for the regulation, control, and distribution of power within the CubeSat. The PDU not only interacts with and establishes connections to other subsystems, but it allows the subsystems to remain powered and functional. The PDU has the most amount of components compared to the rest of the Artemis CubeSat’s PCBs, thus making it one of the most complex subsystems. In the following sections, the components of the PDU, organized by category of its type, are explored.
5.1.1. PC104 Header


The function of the PC104 Header is to connect power and communication lines to other boards in Artemis CubeSat that also have a PC 104 Header. For instance, the Teensy 4.1 on the OBC has a I2C connection that the PDU uses. In order for the OBC and PDU board to share the I2C connection, a PC 104 Header is used. Each PC 104 Header consists of 2 headers, and 52 pins per header. The notation is HX-YZ, where X is the header number (either 1 or 2) and YZ is the pin number (from 1 to 52). The name of a connection may differ depending on the board. However, the connections are one and the same. For example, OBC PC 104 Header H1-1 (Header 1, Pin 1) is connected to UART4_RXD whereas the PDU PC 104 Header H1-1 is connected to UART2_RXD. These connections have different names but are the same lines.

5.1.3. VBatt Filter


The function of the VBatt Filter is to remove (filter) noise from the raw VBattery power line. The result of the VBatt Filter is a filtered, sustainable, and usable battery voltage that is subsequently used to power the CubeSat’s load, e.g. all other devices in the CubeSat. The VBatt Filter implements a simple, passive low pass filter, e.g. a Resistor-Capacitor (R-C) circuit. The low-pass filter is first-order since it only uses one reactive component, in this case, one capacitor. 
The inputted voltage, VBatt, should roughly equal the output voltage, VBatt, with the only difference being the output voltage has significantly less noise than the inputted raw voltage, as shown in Fig. 5.1.3.a. Assuming the pin in the insert before flight (IBF) is removed (Section 5.1.12) and the deployment switch is on (Section 7.4), VBatt will always be on and range between a minimum of 6V and maximum of 7.2V. 
The resistor and capacitor were calculated based on the fact the cut-off frequency was 10 kHz. If they choose to do so, the end-user may choose a different cut-off frequency, and calculate new resistor and capacitor values based on Equation 5.1.3.a.

5.1.5. H-Bridge Circuits


The function of the H-Bridge Circuits is to change the polarization in order to control the current direction of the torque coils on the Solar Panel Boards. Polarization refers to the orientation (direction and magnitude) of a vibrating electric field, or a wave’s oscillation.  It is an attribute of electromagnetic radiation. The H-Bridge is powered by the 3V3 Bus, which is always on when the CubeSat is turned on. 


The importance of the H-Bridge circuits is that it controls the torque coils on the Solar Panel Boards, which aid in the CubeSat’s movement in orbit. There are two torque coils on each solar panel board, with each orienting in opposite directions. When the CubeSat is on and the H-Bridge is powered by the 3V3 Bus, the PDUs Microcontroller sends a digital signal to the H-Bridge Circuit, which tells it how much each torque coil on the solar panel board has to move in order to reach the desired position. The H-Bridge Circuit then outputs a certain amount of current to the torque coils, where the amount of current is related to how much the respective torque coils need to move the CubeSat. When the current direction is changed, the torque coils will cause the CubeSat to slowly move into the correct position as programmed.

5.1.6. 3V3, 5V, VBattery Switches


The switch regulator has two primary functions:
   1. Convert input voltage into a desired voltage, which is then supplied to certain circuits.
   2. Control whether the supplied circuit is switched on or off.


Switches are useful implementations because some devices on the CubeSat should not always be on during certain durations of the satellite mission. For example, breakout power refers to power that is connected to another component outside the PCB. Its usage is mostly for the end-user, and there may be moments one does not want the breakout power to be on.


There are two 3V3 switches. The switches are supplied by the 3V3 Bus and output usable 3.3V (3V3). It is controlled by the PDUs microprocessor. Each switch has a purpose: 
   1. Provide 3V3 breakout power for the end-user.
   2. Provide an available 3V3 switch the end-user can program and utilize.


There are four 5V switches. The switches are supplied by the 5V Bus and output usable 5V. It is controlled by the PDUs microprocessor. Each switch has a purpose: 
   1. Provide 5V power to the Raspberry Pi Zero on the OBC. Recall the Raspberry Pi Zero aids in the controls on the satellite.
   2. Provide 5V power to the Kapton Heater. Recall the Kapton Heater maintains voltage in extreme heat environments.
   3. Provide an available 5V switch the end-user can program and utilize.
   4. Provide 5V power to the 12V Switch Regulator. The 12V Switch Regulator circuit converts the input 5V to output 12V for the end-user to program and utilize.
There is one VBatt switch. The switch is supplied by the filtered VBatt and output usable VBatt. It is controlled by the PDUs microprocessor. Its purpose is to provide an available VBatt switch the end-user can program and utilize.


There is one depletion MOSFET for the 5V Switch #1, which provides power to the Raspberry Pi Zero on the OBC. The purpose of the depletion MOSFET is to drain any excess current between the switch turning on and off. For instance, when the 5V Switch #1 turns off, there remains some current in the circuit. The depletion MOSFET works to get rid of any leftover current in order to decrease the risk of performance degradation.

5.1.8. Burn Wire Circuit


The burn wire circuit is a CubeSat deployment mechanism. Prior to deployment, the antenna is bent (not creased) and tied to the structure of the CubeSat by the burn wire, or a fishing line. The burn wire circuit is powered by VBatt and controlled by the PDUs microprocessor. When it is time for deployment, the CubeSat is turned on, such that VBatt is flowing throughout the CubeSat, and the PDUs microprocessor sends a digital signal to the burn wire circuit, indicating it is time for the circuit to turn on. The burn wire circuit outputs a high voltage, which is connected to a molex connector. The molex connector links together the high voltage and the burn wire located externally surrounding the CubeSat. The high voltage then heats up the burn wire tied around the antenna and structure of the small satellite. When the burn wire is heated high enough, the wire will burn, and the antenna is deployed. For more information on the fundamentals and functionality of the Burn Wire Circuit, please see this Burn Wire Circuit lab.

5.1.9. Microcontroller Unit (MCU)


The function of the microcontroller unit (MCU) is to control several circuits and its functionality throughout the CubeSat. It communicates with the rest of the PCBs, processes data, and more. The end-user may also expand upon the instructions and program the MCU to obtain their desired controls. The MCU is powered by the 3V3 Bus and outputs several digital signals to circuits in order to control their aspects, for instance, when certain circuits should turn on or off. 


The WatchDog Timer was implemented to reset the MCU at certain intervals in order to continue MCU performance. It auto generates a system reset in the case the PDU is stalled. The LED was implemented to visually show when the MCU is turned on and able to communicate with the rest of the CubeSat.

5.1.10. Temperature, Current & Power Sensors


The function of the sensors is to record and detect changes within the PCB and output a response to those changes. The two main sensors featured on the PDU are the:
      1. Temperature Sensor - which provides information on the temperature of the PCB.
      2. Current and Power Sensors - which provide information on the current and power (voltage) flowing through the PCBs. 


The temperature sensor input is the 3V3 Bus. The temperature sensor output is an analog signal, which is used for temperature control. There are several temperature sensors throughout the Artemis CubeSat:
      * 1 on the OBC (AIN0 - Analog Input 0)
      * 1 on the PDU (AIN1 - Analog Input 1)
      * 1 on the Battery Board (AIN2 - Analog Input 2)
      * 4 on the Solar Panel Boards (AIN3 to AIN6 - Analog Input 3 to 6)
      * 1 on each Solar Panel Board


Each temperature sensor’s analog output is connected to OBC’s Teensy 4.1, which sends commands to devices in response to temperature changes. For instance, if the temperature sensor detects that a PCB is too high in temperature then it may turn off some devices on that PCB. When the temperature is back in an operable acceptable range, the Teensy will turn the devices back on.


The current and power sensor input is the 3V3 Bus. The current and power sensor output is I2C data depicting the current and power (voltage) flowing through the PCBs. There are several current and power sensors on the PDU:
      * 1 measures current and power for VBatt, which supplies the primary power to PDUs load
      * 1 measures current and power for each Solar Panel Board Input. Recall there are four solar panel boards so there will be four current and power sensors in total.


Each current and power sensor’s I2C line is connected to OBC’s Teensy 4.1, which sends commands to devices in response to current and power changes. For example, if the current and power sensor reads the current going across some component is not ideal, the Teensy may shut off some devices to allow time for a reset to occur, then turn back on the devices when the current had time to stabilize.

5.1.12. Insert Before Flight (IBF) Circuit


For PDU v2.2, the RBF is now IBF (insert before flight) which only consists of a simple switch. 


The IBF Circuit is a safety mechanism that allows for battery power to be disconnected and reconnected to the CubeSat through the use of a IBF pin. When the pin is connected to the CubeSat, there is a closed circuit so power flows through and the CubeSat does function. When the pin is removed, power does not flow through the CubeSat, and its respective electronic components are turned off. IBF is an important element because it is used for the control, testing, and integration of the small satellite when not in space.
  Fig. 5.1.12. RBF (now IBF) Schematic
        
The BF circuit consists of two N-Channel MOSFETs, U10 and U24. To understand how the IBF works, the following characteristics of MOSFETs should be reviewed:
      1. If a high voltage flows through the gate of a MOSFET, then the source and drain behaves as a short circuit
      2. If no or little voltage flows through the gate of a MOSFET, then the source and drain behaves as an open circuit


The IBF circuit has two main cases:
      1. IBF Pin Inserted
      1. J15’s Pin 1 and 2 are not connected
      2. 0V flows through U10’s Gate Pin (Pin 1)
      3. U10’s Source Pin (Pin 2) and Drain Pin (Pin 3) are open circuits
      4. There is a high voltage flowing across U10’s Source Pin (Pin 2) and U24’s Gate Pin (Pin 1)
      5. U24’s Source Pin (Pin 2) and Drain Pin (Pin 3) are a short circuit, meaning the connections are one and the same
      6. Result is the Battery Power from the PyCubed Battery Board is the same power source that is supplied to the CubeSat load. Therefore, EPS system and hence, CubeSat are powered on
      2. IBF Pin Removed
      1. J15’s Pin 1 and 2 are shorted together and connected
      2. A high voltage flows through U10’s Gate Pin (Pin 1)
      3. U10’s Source Pin (Pin 2) and Drain Pin (Pin 3) are shorted together
      4. There is a no voltage flowing across U10’s Source Pin (Pin 2) and U24’s Gate Pin (Pin 1)
      5. U24’s Source Pin (Pin 2) and Drain Pin (Pin 3) are an open circuit, meaning the connection was not made.
      6. Result is the Battery Power from the PyCubed Battery Board is not connected to the power source that is supplied to the CubeSat load. Therefore, the EPS system and hence, CubeSat are not powered on.

5.3 Solar Panels


Among these critical subsystems, solar panels play a crucial role in powering CubeSats and ensuring their long-duration operation in the harsh environment of space. Solar panels, also referred to as solar arrays or photovoltaic (PV) arrays, harness the energy of the sun and convert it into electrical power. This renewable energy source allows CubeSats to operate autonomously, collect data, communicate with ground stations, and execute their mission objectives.


The Artemis CubeSat’s solar panels contain embedded torque coils which are strategically placed within the structure of the solar panels. These coils generate magnetic fields when electric currents pass through them, thereby exerting torque on the satellite. By selectively activating and modulating the currents in these coils, precise control over the satellite's attitude can be achieved.


The integration of torque coils within the solar panel structure offers several advantages for CubeSat missions. Firstly, it eliminates the need for separate mechanical components such as reaction wheels or magnetic torquers, reducing the overall mass, volume, and complexity of the satellite. This compact design is particularly beneficial for CubeSats, where stringent constraints on size, weight, and power are imposed.


Furthermore, embedding torque coils in the solar panels improves the utilization of available space in the satellite, as the solar panels serve a dual purpose by both generating power and contributing to attitude control. This integration enhances the overall efficiency and functionality of CubeSat missions, allowing for a more optimized use of resources.

8. Mechanical
The Artemis CubeSat, relies on a mechanical subsystem that serves as the backbone of its structural stability and integrity. This pivotal component plays a crucial role throughout the spacecraft's entire lifecycle, navigating through the challenges of harsh launch conditions and the demanding space environment. Tasked with the mechanical design, fabrication, and testing of the CubeSat's frame and housing, this subsystem securely houses internal components.


Designed in adherence to the CubeSat standard of small-sized cubic units (U), with dimensions of 10x10x10 cm, the mechanical subsystem incorporates lightweight yet robust and rigid materials, optimizing the limited space available, ensuring the CubeSat's overall functionality.


The mechanical subsystem of the Artemis CubeSat goes beyond its role in structural support; it is a key player in ensuring compliance with the specifications of the launch vehicle provider. This is particularly critical, as the CubeSat must endure violent vibrations and loads during launch without compromising the safety of the rocket or other payloads. Rigorous structural and thermal analyses, coupled with extensive testing and qualification procedures, validate the robustness of the mechanical design, guaranteeing the spacecraft's reliability under extreme conditions.

4.5mm OD  M3  aluminum spacers are purchased from McMaster Carr in 3mm, 5mm, 12mm, and 20mm lengths. Spacers are placed on the structure rods in order to separate the boards from one another. Spacers are put through structure rods to separate boards.




  

8.2.1.2 Rods


3 mm OD  2.2mm ID 316 stainless steel tubing is used for the structure rods. Each kit uses 4x  97.4 mm long rods with 0.45mm thread cut ¼” deep on each end. Slide in rods through the top section and secure using 8x  M2.5  0.45mm thread  6mm long 90° countersink flat head screws. A screw goes on both ends of each rod to prevent the rods from sliding out of the structure. Refer to V1 Kit Assembly Tutorial for assembly instructions.
8.2.1.3 Antenna


The antenna is cut from ½” wide  0.0040” thick  1095 spring steel. It is attached to the antenna board using 2x  6-32 thread ¼” long pan head screws, 2x  0.38” OD  0.15” ID plastic washers, and 2x  6-32 hex nuts.

	9. TCS
In the harsh environment of outer space, maintaining the optimal temperature range for electronic components is crucial to ensure the functionality and longevity of these small satellites. This is where the thermal control system steps in, acting as the guardian of temperature regulation on a CubeSat. Designed to tackle the challenges posed by extreme temperature fluctuations, limited power, and confined space, the thermal control system plays a vital role in ensuring the mission's success.


9.1 Kapton Heater PLM-203/10-P
The Artemis CubeSat incorporates the Kapton Heater PLM-203/10-P, a compact and vacuum-safe heating device measuring 2" x 3". Powered by a 5 V line, this heater consumes 1.7 W of energy. The heater is strategically positioned atop the battery pack to ensure optimal thermal management.


The PLM-203/10-P is a part of the PLM-Series of polyimide flexible heaters manufactured by Kapton. These heaters are designed to be used in various environments and hard-to-reach places due to their increased flexibility and chemical resistivity​. The PLM-203/10-P model specifically has a width of 2 inches, a length of 3 inches, and is self-adhesive with a watt density of 10 W/in^2​.


Specifications
Performance Ratings
         * Maximum Operating Temperature: 232°C (450°F)
         * Minimum Operating Temperature: -57°C (-71°F)
         * Non-magnetic heating element
         * Watt densities: 10 W/in^2​
Physical Size and Construction Limitations
         * Width: 2 inches
         * Length: 3 inches
         * Thickness: 0.01 inches
         * Wire length tolerance: ±0.4 inches​


Construction
The PLM-203/10-P is made with a thin metal foil (0.001"), usually a nickel base alloy, as the resistance element. The resistance pattern to be etched is designed in CAD and transferred to the foil, which is laminated to the insulating substrate. The element/substrate is then processed through an acid spray to produce the desired resistance pattern. The top layer is then added and laminated. Lead wires are attached to the heater and insulated epoxy cement. The heater can deliver uniform heat profiles with higher watt densities, providing longer operating heater life. It can also be zoned with distributed wattage or separate heating circuits to compensate for load variations​.


Special Features
         * Polyimide strain relief and insulation
         * Optional pressure-sensitive adhesive models available
         * Non-magnetic heating element​
